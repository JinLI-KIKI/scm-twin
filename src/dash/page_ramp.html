Module 6 · new-site ramp and risk board hedge

Ramp Up, Board Up

A new site takes about three months from the first trial build to full volume, and it can be pulled in; material rhythm, yield and supplier readiness are what hold it back. While it ramps, China builds the rest and pays the tariff. On the board side, every SMT run locks CPU and memory into boards: short runs keep material loose for uncertain models, long runs keep the ODM's capacity. Push builds back and the ODM may give the line to another customer, and capacity drops: with a weak GSM that risk is large. Both are simulated live below.

Ramp (R16, R21, R46). From the first trial build the new site follows an S-curve (smoothstep) to full volume over T weeks: T = the base ramp less the weeks pulled in (never below the fastest ramp), plus the slips. Each gate, material rhythm, yield and supplier readiness, slips with its own chance (times the GSM factor) by a uniform number of weeks between its bounds; supplier readiness slips less often on an existing ODM campus than at a new plant, and since slips overlap, their total is capped (Q47). China builds what the new site cannot yet and pays the tariff on its customs value (leakage). Units that fail first pass consume material: yield follows 85% → 91% → 95% → 97% → 98% over the weeks to full yield (about 4 for a mature product moving lines, 6–8 for a new product or new suppliers, Q48), and each failed unit costs a share of its BOM value. Pulling in is paid only on the volume it brings forward: the first week pulled costs $6 per unit moved forward, each further week 50% more, compounding ($6, $9, $13.50, $20.25, Q48). The pull-in with the lowest expected cost is marked.

Board runs (R13, R19, R47). Each SMT run builds boards for the next N weeks of forecast; forecasts get worse with the horizon (accuracy 68% at 16 weeks, falling with its square root) and run high for models that sold badly. A board locks CPU and memory to the model and the site's origin: when the model ends (end of life, or an early cut), boards left over cost their full value as MWD, where loose parts would mostly migrate (Module 5: 90%). The ODM builds about 8 weeks of boards per run by default and the brand can push builds later; runs shorter than the standard cost setups and capacity, and how much depends heavily on the GSM (Q38). In every run the ODM gives a share of the line time it is not loaded with to other customers. On top, each week the model sells there is a chance, rising with how far builds are pushed back, that the ODM moves the model's line to another customer: for about a quarter both builds and rush capacity drop. Short of boards, rush boards cover up to the flex share of the run's planned volume at a premium of a quarter of the board's own price (without CPU and memory), between $3 and $10 a board (Q49); of the units still short, 30% are lost at gross margin and the rest ship late (half and half is the stress case). Boards on hand carry at the carrying rate. The recommended cover has the lowest score, a blend of the expected cost and the P90, so the bad years count too.
